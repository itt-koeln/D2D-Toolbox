### Project information

The Water Security Toolbox in this pilot project is under development in close collaboration with local research partners in **Sudan** and **Ethiopia** including the University of Khartoum, the National water research center-Sudan, and the University of Addis Ababa. In addition to local stakeholders from public institutions, NGOs as well as international development to achieve water security and economic development in that region. The results will later be disseminated to other data scarce regions.

### Background

Climate change will lead to water scarcity and drought in many regions of the world, especially in arid and Least Development Countries in South Sahil and the Nile Basin. At the same time, increasing demand for water will often endanger water resources security and conflicts over water allocation. In many countries, data on water availability, water use, and efficiency are often very poor, so that no adequate decisions can be made for short-term or strategic measures towards increased water security. However, the rapid development of remote sensing, data analysis, modelling capabilities, and advanced communication technologies offer tremendous opportunities to make a quantum leap in water resources management.

The pilot study area is the Dinder and Rahad River Basin, a transboundary basin located in the Blue Nile Basin in Ethiopia and Sudan.

![]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/uploads/2019_05_overviewmap-530x402.jpg)
*Figure 1. An overview map of the study area*

The WaterSec project aims to close the above-mentioned data and knowledge gap by developing and disseminating a scientifically based toolbox for the analysis and assessment of water resource security in data-scarce regions. This includes achieving the following goals:

1. Exploration and comparison of available public domain data on parameters of water availability, water demand, and land use.
2. Use of data for modeling and analysis of climate variability, water balance (water accounting) and drought indicators.
3. Further development of the analysis results for the creation of regional drought scenarios.
4. Compilation and evaluation of adaptation strategies to droughts.
5. Processing knowledge for users through innovative information technology and appropriate communication strategies.
6. Exploitation of scientific results together with project partners and application in pilot regions.

### Current results

- **An inventory was done to collect the basic information about the basin and serve as a base for deep analysis (see the attachment in the following link: [Dinder-Rahad Basins Inventory]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/uploads/2019_05_dinderrahadbasinsinventory.pdf)).**

                                                 ---------------------------

- **Comparison of different open sources precipitation products in the study area:**

Spatial and temporal variation of 9 precipitation products over the study area was assessed to evaluate how different products capture the precipitation in the same period, or over the same area. This study evaluated the following precipitation products: African Rainfall Climatology Version 2.0 (ARC 2.0; Novella and Thiaw, 2013), Climate Hazards Group Infrared Precipitation with station data version 2.0 (CHIRPS 2.0; Funk et al., 2015), Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation version 2.0 (MSWEP 2.0; Beck et al., 2017), Precipitation Estimation from Remotely Sensed Information using Artificial Neural Networks- Climate Data Record (PERSIANN-CDR; Ashouri et al., 2015), Tropical Applications of Meteorology using Satellite data and ground-based observations (TAMSAT; Jobard et al., 2011), the Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission (TRMM; Huffman et al., 2007), the Climate Prediction Center Morphing technique product (CMORPH; Joyce et al. 2004; Xie et al., 2017), the Global Precipitation Climatology Center full data reanalysis version 7.0 (GPCC; Schneider et al. 2015), and the Climatic Research Unit Standardization of Tree-ring data ( CRUST; Melvin and Briffa, 2014).

Further statistical analysis and comparison with ground-based data from stations within and around the study area will be carried out to recommend the best product per region/purpose. Our partners will provide rainfall data from the ground stations. The results of this analysis are shown in figure 3 and Figure 4.

- **Determination of water productivity for different food-related land cover types (i.e Irrigated cropland, Rain-fed cropland, and Closed to open grassland):**

Agriculture water productivity variability, which is the amount of output (Biomass) per cubic meter of water used (FAO, 2012) was assessed by calculating the above-ground Gross Biomass Water Productivity (GBWP, kg/m3) and Net Biomass Water productivity (NBWP, kg/m3). Those indicators provide insights on the impact of vegetation development on consumptive water use and thus on water balance over the study area (FAO, 2018) by monitoring the performance of irrigated and rain-fed agriculture in relation to water consumption. The results of this analysis are shown in Figure 13 and Figure 14.

Based on FAO (2018), the GBWP is the ratio of the annual above-ground biomass production (kg/ha) per annual actual evapotranspiration (m3/ha). While the NBWP is the ratio of kg of dry matter per cubic meter of water transpired by vegetation in one hectare.

- **Calculation of some drought indicators in the basin based on remote sensing data:**

Some selected drought indices such as Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI; Mckee et al., 1993) and the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010) which measure the duration and severity of drought event, and the Water Deficit Index (WDI, Moran et al., 1994) were calculated using remote sensing data sets. Scripts for automatic download and process of the required data using R programing language were developed to ease the work.
